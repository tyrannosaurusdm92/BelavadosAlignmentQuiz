Naming and replacement rules used in this document
The bright band crossing the sky is called the Star-River of Belavadös.
The observing landscape is described as the Great River Valley and its desert-edge skyfields.
The twelve wandering planets and two moons use the supplied invented names and coordinates.
The sky analysis keeps the historical-style latitude, precession, seasonality, and horizon logic, but is written as Belavadös sky lore rather than a modern archaeological report.
Coordinate system used
The sky is described with right ascension and declination. These coordinates function like celestial longitude and latitude. Apparent magnitude uses lower numbers for brighter objects, with negative values being especially bright. Planet and moon distances use AU or kilometers; stellar distances use light-years.
Historical-style sky setting
The analysis assumes a view from approximately 30° N latitude. At 30° N, stars above about +60° declination are circumpolar, while stars below about -60° declination never rise.
The sky looks dark, dense, and sharply structured. With no electric skyglow, the Star-River of Belavadös would have been a major visible feature rather than a faint haze. Bright planets, bright stars, both moons, and the eight named constellations would have served as seasonal markers, navigation guides, calendar anchors, and story-bearing landmarks.
The Star-River of Belavadös
The Star-River of Belavadös is the luminous band of unresolved stars and dark rifts crossing the night sky. In summer evenings, it would stretch through the southern gate-and-serpent sectors and climb toward the northern seasonal markers. In winter evenings, it would run near the Bronze Bull region, becoming paler but still visible under dry, dark conditions.
From desert-edge skyfields, the Star-River would not have appeared as a smooth strip. It would have looked mottled, braided, and broken by dark channels. The brightest warm-season section would have been low-to-mid in the southern sky; the higher northern section would have served as a nightly path for visual navigation.

Eiršu'neth Comet
The Eiršu'neth Comet is a massive, long-period Vishtecu–Hyperu family comet distinguished by its unusually large icy nucleus, high mass, and stable yet dramatic inclined orbit through the outer Belavadös system. With a nucleus about 85 miles (137 km) wide—roughly 10–50 times larger than a typical comet core—and an estimated mass around 500 trillion tons (about 500× that of an average comet), it is one of the most visually and dynamically significant comets in its region. It follows a repeating 6.48 Belavadös-year orbit (≈ 2,139.37 Belavadös days), with a semi-major axis of ~3.48 AU, ranging from a perihelion of 2.08 AU to an aphelion of 4.87 AU. Its 18° inclination above the Celestial Road and carefully positioned crossing points make it a safe, non-impacting body despite its size, periodically producing a spectacular multi-month display as it passes through the inner reaches of its orbit.

Seasonal sky analysis
Winter evenings: The Minotaur Labyrinth ascends
Winter evenings are dominated by The Minotaur Labyrinth of Anubaldir. Anubaldir Stone and Minotaur Eye mark the inner maze, while Goliath Horn and Firbolg Horn rise as paired tips above the bull-headed figure. Cyclopian Watch and Giff Burden form the lower gate and shoulder, making the shape read as both beast and maze.
At 30° N, the Labyrinth's upper horn stars climb high while its lower gate remains mid-sky. Because the six stars sit between roughly RA 04h 51m and 06h 12m, the figure stays compact enough for a generator to draw a recognizable horned head rather than a scattered seasonal region.
Spring evenings: Mirror, Web, and Twin Drums
Spring is the richest named-constellation season. The Luma Mirror of Nephthysra, The Silkborn Web of Thoryn-Rahek, and The Twin Drums of Ishtanora all become prominent. Their centers are deliberately separated in RA while their internal stars remain tightly grouped, allowing each figure to resolve as its own shape.
This season is excellent for calendar work. The repeated appearance of three interlinked spring figures gives sky-watchers a stable sequence: the Web high in the north, the Mirror through the middle sky, and the Twin Drums balancing the equatorial zone.
Summer evenings: The Deep Gate and southern Star-River
Summer brings the Sahuagin Deep Gate of Bastveig into the southern sky. Its upper lintel, twin pillars, and lower threshold span roughly RA 16h 01m to 17h 03m and Dec -18° to -46°, so the shape reads as a tall doorway rather than a diagonal scatter.
The Star-River of Belavadös is most visually complex during this season. It would look bright, broken, and braided through the southern skyfields, then lift toward northern seasonal markers. The Deep Gate is deliberately horizon-sensitive, making it an oceanic and trench-associated marker.
Late summer and autumn: Crossroads Mask, Serpent, and Crown
Late summer brings The Crossroads Mask of Freyseth low in the southern sky. The paired eye stars, hooked smile stars, and lower road-post star create a mask-like figure that is visible but vulnerable to haze, matching its lore of thresholds, temptation, and uncertain identity.
As the cooling season advances, The Dragonborn Dominion of Valkhamesh and The Wechselkind Crown of Setrimir become defining figures. The Dominion forms a curving serpent at northern autumn declinations, while the Crown arcs around the 0h right-ascension seam without breaking the figure for a coordinate generator.
Circumpolar and horizon behavior
At 30° N, the circumpolar threshold is approximately +60° declination. The revised named constellation stars remain below that threshold, so they rise and set rather than staying permanently visible. This preserves seasonal motion while still allowing high northern figures to climb impressively.
The southern horizon limit is approximately -60° declination. The lowest revised named stars remain above that limit, so the Deep Gate and Crossroads Mask are visible from the assumed latitude, though dust, humidity, terrain, and haze can hide their faintest lower stars.
Representative night: paired moons and wanderers
A representative high-era observing night can be imagined with Chandratecu-Seleneš and Mictlindra-Nannara close together near RA 06h 15m and Dec +18° to +19°. This places the twin lunar system near the winter-to-spring celestial fields. The moonlet would appear close to the larger lunar disk, sometimes separated by a small visible gap and sometimes crossing as a bead-like shadow.
When the bright inner planet Mardukesh-Huitra is favorably placed, it dominates the evening or dawn sky with pearl-white brilliance. Surixoch-Aresu appears copper-red and lower near twilight. The four ringed planets are not equally easy to interpret with the unaided eye, but under magnification their ring lore becomes central: Vishtecu-Hyperu shows a rose-gold hairline, Enlimitra a frosted silver span, Quetzal-Hermeshu a tilted green-bronze eye, and Varunatl-Kronu a white-ringed blue elongation.

Practical observing guide for Belavadös sky-watchers
For winter navigation, begin with The Minotaur Labyrinth of Anubaldir: the two horn tips and maze-heart create the clearest cold-season figure.
For spring calendars, track the rising sequence of The Silkborn Web of Thoryn-Rahek, The Luma Mirror of Nephthysra, and The Twin Drums of Ishtanora.
For summer deep-sky watching, follow the Star-River of Belavadös through the southern skyfields toward The Sahuagin Deep Gate of Bastveig.
For late-summer horizon work, watch The Crossroads Mask of Freyseth; its lower road-post star is an excellent test of sky clarity.
For autumn ceremonial timing, use The Wechselkind Crown of Setrimir and The Dragonborn Dominion of Valkhamesh as high and mid-northern markers.
For lunar calendars, record both the broad phase of Chandratecu-Seleneš and the rapid position changes of Mictlindra-Nannara.
Source and method notes
The astronomical logic in this document uses standard sky-coordinate concepts: right ascension, declination, apparent magnitude, latitude-dependent visibility, horizon limits, and axial precession. The revised constellation placements use the attached all-sky constellation map and IAU solid-angle/center references as coordinate scaffolding only. Earth star names were not used; all fixed-star names are Belavadös deity/race-based, and every constellation has been remapped so its listed stars sit close enough together to form the described figure in a sky generator.
No Earth star names are included. The document is intended as a lore-compatible sky reference for Belavadös and as a practical coordinate table for generators; the fixed stars are deliberately placed at remote background distances and several are faint enough to be only partially visible without unusually dark skies or magnification.
