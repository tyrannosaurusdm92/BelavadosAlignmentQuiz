A complete guide for players and Dungeon Masters
By William Saville, also known as The Transgender T-Rex #TheTransgenderTRex
I built this system to make alignment feel alive instead of frozen. Belavadös treats morality as a profile instead of a label, so characters can grow, slide, fail, recover, and surprise the table in a way that still stays easy to read.
This guide is written for Dungeon Masters, veteran D&D; players who know the old alignment grid, and new players who need a clean way in. I keep the language direct, the rules visible, and the moving parts organized so the system can be learned without losing its depth.
Nothing here is meant to trap a character in a box. Your choices shape your path, and your path reshapes your destiny.
How I want you to use this guide
For DMs, the recommendation is to start with the core axes, then move into the phase rules, and finally the XP sections. These pages establish how to run the system consistently and fairly at the table. For veteran players, the conversion material translates the old two-axis model into the newer four-axis profile system, preserving familiar ideas while adding precision and reducing ambiguity around labels. For new players, the first half of the guide is sufficient to begin play—focus on the axes, review the examples, and understand the difference between fit awards and shift awards. The appendices function as reference material you can consult when needed during play.
What I built this system to do
The system is designed to measure selfishness, lawfulness, cooperation, and honor as separate and independent questions rather than collapsing them into a single alignment label. This allows a character to be lawful yet selfish, cooperative yet dishonorable, or honorable while actively rejecting institutions. The result is a clearer moral shape that is easier to understand and roleplay.
It also emphasizes long-term behavioral patterns over isolated actions. A single act of kindness does not elevate a character into sainthood, just as a single cruel decision does not erase a lifetime of decency. Instead, Belavadös tracks patterns, turning points, sacrifices, betrayals, and repeated behavior to build a more stable and meaningful moral profile.

What I built and why it is different
Belavadös treats morality as a living profile rather than a single alignment label. Instead of compressing ethical identity into a fixed nine-box system like traditional D&D alignment, it evaluates a character through four independent axes. Each axis measures a different moral or behavioral pressure, allowing for more nuance and less forced categorization.
Belavadös Moral Axes
This separation is important because real characters rarely fit cleanly into a single moral box. A selfish mercenary is not the same as a dishonorable traitor. A lawful bureaucrat is not automatically cooperative. A cooperative rebel is not necessarily altruistic, and an honorable outlaw may still reject institutional authority entirely. By keeping these axes distinct, Belavadös preserves moral complexity instead of flattening it into broad labels.
The system is also designed to reflect change over time. Each axis can shift as a character grows, deteriorates, or reacts to events. This turns morality into a dynamic record rather than a static label—allowing the system to track a character’s moral biography as they develop, rather than assigning them a single permanent identity.
The four alignment axes
Every character in Belavadös is measured across four independent moral dimensions. Each axis runs from 0 to 3000 points, and 1500 is the neutral center.
If you only remember one thing from this page, remember this: the axes are separate. A character can be high on one axis and low on another without contradiction. That is the heart of the system.

Character creation and starting scores
At character creation, or through birth, adoption, awakening, or transformation in game, I let players start in more than | one way. They may take a short scenario based alignment test, choose their alignment manually, or build from a | background, oath, faction, or backstory that clearly points in a direction.
The default starting point for every axis is 1500. That means a new character begins at the neutral center unless the | fiction says otherwise. A DM may shift starting scores by about 100 to 300 points for an ordinary background, and by | larger amounts when divine influence, faction indoctrination, or a very strong concept justifies it.

I use those modifiers to create distinct personalities without permanently locking anyone into a rigid moral box. The goal is to give the character a believable starting shape, then let play do the rest.
Alignment is dynamic
Your actions continuously shape your moral trajectory. I do not treat alignment as something a player gets stuck with forever. I treat it as something that evolves through play.
A character can begin in one place and end up somewhere very different after one meaningful betrayal, sacrifice, or turning point. That is not a bug in the system. That is the point of it.
In play, I usually think in patterns. One kind act matters, but a repeated pattern of sacrifice matters more. One cruel | choice matters, but a repeated habit of exploitation matters more. The score should reflect the true story of the | character, not a single scene taken in isolation.
Why alignment matters at the table
Belavadös is not just a label system. It is a living reputation system. When the table uses it well, the world can answer the characters in different ways depending on how they have actually behaved.
That means the system can influence trust, fear, admiration, hostility, political standing, access to sacred spaces, rare dialogue, faction invitations, and the kind of legacy people speak about after the session ends.
I want the world to react to the character in distinct ways, not just say they are good or evil. That is why I separate honor from lawfulness and cooperation from altruism.

That makes it easier to model gradual | change, mixed motives, and morally complicated characters while still staying fully compatible with D&D; 5e roleplay, | factions, magic items, subclasses, divine systems, and reputation mechanics.

Rewarding alignment play
I use the same reward logic for both advancement modes. The difference is not what gets rewarded. The difference is | when the points are handed out. My rule of thumb is simple. If a choice is merely on brand, it gets a fit award. If it | genuinely changes the character's direction, it gets a shift award.
A small repeatable behavior should not create a massive shift by itself. A meaningful scene choice should matter. A major moral decision should matter a lot. A campaign defining sacrifice, betrayal, or oath should matter even more.
I also keep the table honest by tying rewards to visible narrative consequences, not to table noise or repetitive point | farming.
Adventuring XP mode
Adventuring XP is the granular version. I use it when the table likes seeing immediate progress and wants alignment to feel like part of the normal rhythm of play.
I award points at the end of each notable scene. I only award for choices with risk, cost, or public consequence. I do | not award for filler talk, repeated probing, or harmless chatter. The system should reward meaningful roleplay, not | table noise.
When a choice fits the current profile, I count that as a fit award. When the choice changes the profile in a way that matters later, I count that as a shift award. A single scene can earn both, but only when both parts are true.
Milestone XP mode
Milestone XP is the chapter based version. I use it when the campaign is narrative heavy, the table is open ended, or the group wants less bookkeeping during a session.
Milestone mode solves the bookkeeping problem without changing the reward logic. I still reward the same kinds of alignment moments, but I log them during play and hand them out at the end of the session, chapter, or story beat.
That keeps the table moving while still making the alignment system feel alive and responsive.
Axis triggers
These triggers are the practical heart of the system. When I look at a scene, I ask which axis the choice touches and whether the action is pushing the character up or down that axis.

A clean DM rule and the veteran player conversion guide
I use alignment as a living reputation system, not a punishment system. I award points only when actions meaningfully | impact story, relationships, faction standing, moral consequences, or character identity. I avoid rewarding repetitive | behavior farming.
For veteran players, the old two axis model still helps as a memory aid. I just split the old idea into four questions so it becomes easier to read mixed characters.
A traditional lawful good hero will usually read as lawful, altruistic, cooperative, and honorable. A chaotic good hero | will usually read as lawless, altruistic, cooperative, and honorable. A character that was simply neutral in the old model | can land in many different places here, because neutral in the old system often meant the label was not trying to | describe that axis very strongly.
New player quick start and DM guardrails
For new players, the easiest way to use this system is to play your character honestly, say why they are acting when the choice matters, and ask me which axis a decision touches if you are unsure.
If two players make the same choice for different reasons, the motive can matter. Keep the table consistent and explain the rule before the campaign starts.
My fairness rule is simple. If the table can explain the award in one sentence, the award is probably clear enough. If it takes five sentences, I log the moment and settle it at the end of the session.
I do not punish imperfect roleplay. I reward intention and consequence. The goal is to help players explore the system, not to turn the table into a test they can fail by accident.

Final philosophy
Belavadös is not about assigning permanent labels. It is about consequences, reputation, ideology, personal evolution, social perception, and moral complexity.
Characters are remembered not for what box they were placed in, but for the choices they made when it mattered most. That is the idea I wanted this system to preserve.
If you are a DM, I want this guide to be easy to run. If you are a veteran player, I want it to feel familiar but sharper. If you are new to the table, I want it to feel readable instead of intimidating. That is why I built the system the way I did.
